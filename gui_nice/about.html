Dual-Pool Mining Proxy (DPMP)

DPMP is a lightweight, high-reliability Stratum v1 mining proxy designed to sit between one or more miners and two upstream mining pools. It was designed primarily for Bitcoin solo-miners who run one or more ASIC miners (SHA-256) and want to split hash power across compatible coins/pools (i.e., BTC/BCH/DGB). Some miners, like the NerdQAxe++, have a built-in "dual pool mining" feature but the majority of miners out there do not.

So if you have one or more miners that do NOT have a built-in dual-pool mining feature (such as the Canaan Avalon Q or the Nano3S) and you would like, for example, to mine to a BTC pool and a Bitcoin Cash pool simultaneously, then DPMP is for you. 

DPMP enables deterministic dual-pool scheduling, advanced difficulty/extranonce handling, and observability via Prometheus metrics — while remaining simple to deploy and operate.

At a high level, DPMP:

  • Accepts Stratum connections from miners (acts like a pool)
  • Maintains concurrent Stratum connections to two upstream pools (acts like a miner)
  • Routes jobs and share submissions according to a scheduler with manual or auto-balancing
  • Ensures correct propagation of:
    • difficulty
    • extranonce
    • job IDs
  • Exposes runtime metrics for monitoring and analysis
  • Logs structured, machine-readable events for debugging and auditing

The proxy is intentionally transparent: miners and pools do not need to be modified or aware that DPMP is in the middle.

Please visit the DPMP landing page at: https://ckryza.github.io/dpmpv2/.

Fleet-Aware Scheduling

DPMP's scheduler uses a fleet-aware assignment strategy that evaluates each miner's hashrate, health score, and firmware capabilities to determine the optimal role for every miner in the fleet. Rather than switching all miners simultaneously between pools — which causes dramatic ratio oscillations, reject storms, and unnecessary wear on sensitive devices — the scheduler divides the fleet into Static and Dynamic categories.

Small miners are assigned statically to whichever pool needs their hashrate to meet the target weight ratio, where they mine continuously without ever switching. Only the minimum number of miners necessary are designated as Dynamic time-slicers, and these are chosen based on hashrate fit and health score to ensure the most capable and reliable miners handle the switching workload.

This approach eliminates the "herding behavior" of the original implementation, where the entire fleet would stampede from one pool to the other and back again, producing wild swings in the actual ratio and floods of rejected shares. Instead, the target ratio is maintained smoothly by one or two Dynamic miners alternating between pools on calculated time intervals, while the rest of the fleet mines undisturbed.

The result is faster convergence to the target weights, dramatically lower fleet-wide reject rates, and graceful handling of mixed fleets containing miners with varying capabilities and firmware quirks.

Pool Compatibility and Miner Protection

DPMP presents itself as a ckpool-compatible Stratum v1 proxy to all connected miners, regardless of what upstream pool types are configured. This means your miners see a single, consistent mining endpoint — they never need to know that Pool A might be running MiningCore while Pool B runs Bassin (ckpool). Behind the scenes, DPMP handles the translation between different pool behaviors, including differences in difficulty management (VarDiff), extranonce formatting, job delivery timing, and share validation. Each miner maintains its own independent connection to both upstream pools through DPMP, and the scheduler seamlessly routes work and shares to the correct pool based on the current allocation weights.

From the miner's perspective, it is simply connected to one stable pool that occasionally sends new work — exactly as a direct ckpool-type connection would behave.

Automatic Miner Compatibility Detection

Some miners — particularly those running certain firmware like Braiins OS — cannot handle mid-session changes to their extranonce parameters, which is required when switching between pools that use different extranonce sizes (for example, a 4-byte pool and an 8-byte pool).

When DPMP detects this incompatibility, it adapts automatically. On the first pool switch attempt, if a miner accumulates 10 or more consecutive rejected shares with zero accepts, DPMP flags that miner's IP as extranonce-sensitive and immediately reconnects it back to its original pool.

On all subsequent switch attempts, rather than trying to change the extranonce mid-session (which the miner would ignore, producing invalid shares), DPMP gracefully closes the miner's connection and sets a handshake hint pointing to the target pool. The miner reconnects within about one second, performs a fresh handshake directly with the new pool, and begins mining immediately with the correct parameters — no rejected shares, no manual intervention. A 30-second cooldown between forced reconnects provides an additional safety net against excessive disconnects. This entire process is transparent to the miner and requires no configuration; DPMP learns each miner's capabilities and adjusts its switching strategy accordingly.

Dynamic Miner Reject Rates and Pool Switching

Miners assigned to the "Dynamic" (time_slice) category will typically show a higher reject rate in their individual dashboards compared to statically-assigned miners. This is a normal and expected side effect of how DPMP operates. When a Dynamic miner receives a pool switch command, it must flush its internal work pipeline — any shares it has already computed but not yet submitted were built using the previous pool's job data, difficulty, and extranonce parameters. These in-flight shares are essentially orphaned the moment the switch occurs, because the new pool has no knowledge of the old pool's work templates. The new pool will reject these stale shares as invalid, even though the miner performed legitimate work.

From the miner's perspective, it did everything correctly; the rejects are simply a consequence of the pool context changing underneath it. DPMP mitigates this by suppressing many of these transient rejects internally — intercepting them before they reach the miner and returning fake "accepted" responses — but a small number may still pass through during the brief transition window.

Importantly, these rejects do not represent lost hashrate or wasted electricity in any meaningful sense; the miner immediately begins productive work on the new pool within one to two seconds of the switch.

The Relationship Between Slice Duration and Reject Rate

There is a direct inverse relationship between the "Slice Seconds" configuration value and the reject rate seen on Dynamic miners. Each pool switch carries a roughly fixed cost of a few rejected shares during the transition, regardless of how long the miner stays on a given pool. If "Slice Seconds"" is set low (for example, 10 seconds), the miner switches more frequently, and those fixed-cost rejects accumulate as a larger percentage of total shares submitted.

Conversely, a higher "Slice Seconds" (for example, 30 seconds) means fewer switches per hour and a proportionally lower reject rate, since the miner spends more time doing productive work between each transition. For most configurations, a "Slice Seconds" value between 20 and 30 seconds strikes a good balance between responsive weight tracking and acceptable reject rates, typically keeping the Dynamic miner's dashboard reject rate below 2-3%.

It is important to keep in mind when configuring DPMP that the higher the "Slice Seconds" value, the more stable the miner's performance will be (e.g., lower reject percentage), but it will take longer for the system to respond to pool ratio changes. We recommend that you start with a value of 10 for the "Slice Seconds" setting. If you see a spike in reject rate on any Dynamic miner, consider increasing the "Slice Seconds" value to 20 or 25 to reduce the frequency of pool switches. The valid range for "Slice Seconds" is between 10 and 30 seconds.

Hashrate Allocation - Dynamic

Dynamic Hashrate Allocation: with the slider on the Home tab, you can adjust the hashrate allocation (weighting) in realtime with no restart required. Just drag the slider to the desired position and DPMP will adjust accordingly. Some limitations do apply. The slider ranges from 5% to 95% so you cannot allocate 100% of the hashrate to one pool or the other. If you DO want either 0/100 or 100/0 weightings, you must set that in the scheduler section on the Configuration tab and restart DPMP. And if your weighting is set to either 0/100 or 100/0, the Hashrate Allocation slider will no longer appear on the Home tab. Bottom line, though, if you need to temporarily move some hashrate from one pool to the other, the Hashrate Allocation slider is the best way to do that.

Hashrate Allocation - Auto-Balance

Auto-Balancing: if you enable the "Auto Balance" feature in the scheduler section on the Configuration tab, DPMP will automatically adjust the pool weighting ratio periodically based on the observed realtime network hashrate of the coin associated with each pool. The default pool ratio when in "Auto-Balancet" mode is 50/50 and it adjusts from there. The goal of this feature is to send more hashrate to the pool where our hashrate has more "value". So, for example, if BTC short-term network hashrate increases relative to its long-term hashrate while BCH hashrate decreases then DPMP will automatically adjust the weighting to send more hashrate to BCH and less to BTC.

The hashrate information is obtained by polling an oracle API endpoint at regular intervals (default is every 10 minutes) and the oracle data is used to calculate the optimal pool weighting ratio based on the relative hashrate changes. The oracle URL, poll interval, and max deviation threshold can all be configured in the scheduler section on the Configuration tab. When the Auto-Balancer feature is enabled, the Hashrate Allocation slider on the Home tab will no longer be visible since the pool weighting will be automatically managed by DPMP. In its place a new panel will appear showing the current oracle-based pool weighting ratio as well as the realtime network hashrate for each pool. You can also see the oracle data in the Prometheus metrics (ORACLE_HASHRATE, ORACLE_RATIO, ORACLE_WEIGHT, ORACLE_STATUS, and ORACLE_AGE).

Now, our "baseline" network hashrate calculation tracks closely with what you'd see on hashrate reporting sites (e.g., CoinWarz), while the short-term window deliberately captures block-to-block variance to detect transient hashrate shifts — the Auto-Balancer acts on the ratio between the two, not the raw numbers.

Some limitations do apply when using the Auto-Balance feature. Currently, we only support BTC and BCH pools. In the Pool A and B sections of your configuration file you must specify a chain name for each pool (e.g., "BTC" or "BCH"). So, if you are using a combination of pools that are not BTC/BCH (or BCH/BTC) then you won't be able to use the Auto-Balance feature, but you can always use the Hashrate Allocation slider to adjust the weighting in realtime. We also limit the max deviation threshold to between 5 and 45. So with a setting of 5 the Auto-Balancer will never move beyond a 45/55 - 55/45 ratio. With a max deviation threshold of 45, the Auto-Balancer will never move beyond a 5/95 - 95/5 ratio. The default max deviation value is 20, so the Auto-Balancer will never move beyond 30/70 - 70/30.

Note: You can toggle between the Slider panel and the Auto-Balancer panel at any time by clicking on the Slider/Oracle button, and the hashrate allocation will adjust accordingly. Also, the network hashrate charts only update if the data points (e.g. short-term and long-term) change. So even though the default polling interval is every 10 minutes, the charts may not update on every polling cycle if the network hashrate values do not change. If one of the values change, the charts will definitely update.

Pool Failover Protection

DPMP has pool failover protection implemented as follows: if you are splitting work between pools A and B (i.e., pool weighting ratio is NOT 0/100 or 100/0) then if either pool fails, all work (100%) will be routed to the pool that is still active. When the inactive pool becomes available again, the original weighting logic will resume. If BOTH pools become unavailable, DPMP will simply wait until one or both pools become available again and, during this time, it will maintain a connection with any miners attached. If you are currently routing to only one pool (i.e., pool weighting is 0/100 or 100/0) then if the active pool becomes unavailable, DPMP will simply wait until the pool becomes available again...in this situation it will NOT route any work to the pool with the 0 weighting.

Configuration and Setup

When DPMP first starts, you will need to go to the Config tab to edit the pools section and, for both pools, put in the URL/IP Address, Port, and the wallet address to be used when mining to that pool (make sure the wallet address is correct!) You can also give the pool a name. 

By detault, the work will be split 50/50 between the two pools. You can adjust the weighting in the scheduler section (e.g., perhaps 70/30, or 90/10...whatever you like), or you can leave it at 50/50 and use the Hashrate Allocation slider on the Home tab to adjust the weighting in realtime. If you want to use the Auto-Balancer feature, be sure to enable it in the scheduler section, and make sure you have set up a BTC and BCH pool as described in the Auto-Balance section above.

Lastly, check the value of "Slice Seconds" in Scheduler Settings. Start with a value of 10 seconds and give that a try. If you see too many rejects on your Dynamic miner dashboards, then raise the value in 5-second increments until the reject percentage stabilizes.

Once you make the configuration changes, click the APPLY + RESTART button and DPMP will restart. When it does, check the Home tab and make sure you see: DPMP: running, and the tables on the Stats tab should begin to populate.

Now, for each miner you want to use with the proxy, set up the miner's Pool URL to point to the IP address of the box on which you are running the DPMP proxy, set the Port to 3351, and put in one of your wallet addresses, a period, and finally the workername (i.e., <wallet address>.<workername>)

Please note that the wallet address you input in the miner is NOT used by the proxy...only the workername is used. The proxy gets the correct wallet address to use for each pool from the DPMP Configuration file, and when it communicates with either pool it uses the correct wallet address for that pool. So, for example, if you are setting up DPMP to mine to a regular Bitcoin pool and to a Bitcoin Cash pool, in your miner config use either your Bitcoin wallet address or your Bitcoin Cash wallet address (does not matter which) and then append the workername to the end as shown above.

Once your miners are configured and restarted, check the Home tab and you should see the correct number of miners connected, and the Accepted, Rejected, and Jobs stats should be updating. You should also see your miner(s) show up on your pool dashboards as well as in the DPMP Worker Table on the Stats tab.

Note: When using DPMP, it is normal for the Rejection Rate reported in your miner dashboard to be a bit higher than usual, and this is because switching between pools introduces some job not found as well as some stale job conditions. Again, this is normal and nothing to be worried about.

How Many Miners can DPMP Support

I run my copy of DPMP on a AMD Ryzen 5 3550H with 16GB RAM and a 1TB SSD under umbrelOS 1.5 and this setup should easily support up to 50 miners with a hashrate range from 1 to 200TH/s. Now, higher hashrate miners actually produce fewer shares per second (pool VarDiff scales up). A 1 PH/s miner at diff 65536 submits roughly 1 share every 4 seconds. So a farm of 100 x 300 TH/s miners might only generate ~50 shares/sec total, which is very manageable. The ceiling isn't raw CPU — it's the single-threaded event loop getting saturated by JSON processing and share routing.

Recommendations:

Farm Size Miners Total Hashrate Hardware
Small 2 - 30 Up to 5 PH/s Raspberry Pi 5 (8 GB) or any mini-PC
Medium 30 - 75 5 - 50 PH/s 4-core (Ryzen 5 / Intel i5), 16 GB RAM
Large 75 - 200 50 - 200 PH/s 8-core (Ryzen 7 / Intel i7), 32 GB RAM
XL 200 - 500 200+ PH/s 12+ core, 64 GB RAM, or split across 2 DPMP instances

Things to keep in mind:

  • Upstream pool connection limits — Some pools cap connections per IP/wallet. Each miner in DPMP creates a separate upstream connection per pool, so 100 miners = 200 upstream connections (100 to Pool A, 100 to Pool B). Public pools may throttle this. Self-hosted pools don't have this issue.
  • The real scaling path is multiple DPMP instances, each handling a subset of miners. Since DPMP is lightweight, you could run 3-4 instances on the same hardware, each on a different port, splitting the fleet. This also adds redundancy.
  • Log volume scales linearly with miners. At 200 miners, the JSON logging can become significant disk I/O, so suppress all high-frequency events in production.

Error Reporting

If you encounter errors associated either with a particular miner or a particular pool:

  • Please make sure that ALL events are being logged on the Config tab
  • Click APPLY + RESTART to restart DPMP and recreate the error
  • Click on the Download Log button on the Logs tab
  • Send a description of the problem (i.e., miner-related or pool-related and identify the miner or pool) to dpmp.project@gmail.com and attach the log file.

We have tested DPMP with a number of different miners and pools but it is by no means an exhaustive list. The log data you provide will help us to expand the list of supported miners and pools.
